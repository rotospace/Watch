# Cold Outreach

Built by feeding `/watch` a transcript of a short B2B sales talk on cold
outreach structure. No frames were sampled for this one -- the source
came in as pasted transcript text rather than a video file, so treat
"what was on screen" as an unfilled gap; everything here is
audio/text-only. Timestamps below cite where in that transcript each
idea came from.

## The framework [00:00:20-00:02:56]

Four moves, in order, for a first touch:

1. **Open with a credibility hook, not a cold intro** [00:00:20-00:01:00].
   Ideally a real referral ("X suggested I reach out"). When there's no
   referral yet, the same slot can be filled by something specific and
   true about the prospect -- noticed them at an event, noticed
   something on their site, noticed a competitor move that affects
   them. The point is that the first line proves you're not a mail
   merge.

2. **Lead with their problem, not your pitch** [00:01:00-00:01:34].
   Never open with what you do or how great your product is -- name
   the problem the prospect likely has. This is what makes the message
   relevant to them instead of about you, and it's what creates the
   "I should probably do something about this" reaction that gets a
   reply.

3. **Back the problem with a concrete example** [00:01:34-00:01:53].
   A short reference to how a comparable business hit the same problem
   and did something about it lands better than an abstract claim.
   Specificity is what makes it credible.

4. **Ask for a conversation, not a sale** [00:01:53-00:02:56].
   The only thing a first-touch message should "close" is a next
   conversation -- not a purchase, not even a full pitch. Give
   something of real value in the message itself (an observation, a
   useful nugget, something genuinely helpful about their business)
   rather than pushing for the meeting outright. The deal gets closed
   later, in that conversation -- the message's only job is to earn it.

## Applying this to front9's competitor-intel offer

Target: local trades businesses (starting from the epoxy-flooring
pilot; flooring, landscaping, roofing, HVAC, and similar local-service
categories are the likely expansion set -- these owners already track
what competitors charge, so the problem is native to how they think).

**Hook options, in priority order:**
- A real referral from the pilot customer or a mutual contact.
- Something specific noticed about *their* competitive situation --
  e.g. a competitor's ad, pricing page, or promo that's directly
  relevant to them. This doubles as a free preview of the offer.
- A local/industry-specific reference (a trade association, a local
  chamber, an event) if no referral exists yet.

**Problem framing (not the pitch):** most local trades owners are
setting prices and running promos with no real visibility into what
competitors are actually doing right now -- they're guessing, or
finding out after a customer mentions it. Say that, not "we sell an AI
competitive intelligence agent."

**Proof:** reference the pilot (with permission) or a plausible,
specific scenario -- "a flooring company down the street adjusted
pricing within a week of a competitor's promo because they had
visibility into it" is more persuasive than "AI-powered insights."

**Value-in-the-message, not a pitch-in-the-message:** lead with one
real, specific observation about *that* prospect's competitive
landscape if you have it (a competitor's current promo, a pricing gap,
a review trend) -- give away a taste of the insight itself.

**Ask:** a short call to see if it's useful for them -- never "sign up"
or "buy" in the first message.

## Using this skill

When asked to draft outreach for front9:
1. Identify what's actually known about the specific prospect (referral
   available? a real competitor observation to open with? industry
   event in common?) -- use the best hook available, don't default to
   a generic opener.
2. State their likely problem in one or two sentences, in their
   language, not company-pitch language.
3. Add one concrete, specific example or observation -- never an
   abstract claim.
4. Close by asking for a short conversation, and include one genuine,
   specific piece of value in the message itself.
5. Never write a message that tries to close a sale on first touch --
   if a draft mentions pricing, packages, or "let's get started" in the
   first message, cut it.
6. Draft a 2-3 message follow-up sequence alongside the opener, each
   still leading with value, not a repeated pitch.
